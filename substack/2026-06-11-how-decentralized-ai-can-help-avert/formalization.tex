\begin{abstract}
We formalize Ben Goertzel's ``How Decentralized AI Can Help Avert
Bioterrorism'' (2026-06-11) within the Hyperseed ontology, incorporating
d-calculus extensions from Hyperseed v2. The bits-vs-atoms asymmetry
is modeled as information-physical fiber curvature, decentralized
verification as distributed fiber inspection with coverage curvature,
scoped reputation as multi-fiber trust with reputation curvature,
neural-symbolic screening as generate-and-verify with screening
holonomy, and biosurveillance as an immune system with a surveillance
gradient measuring coverage uniformity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Information fiber vs physical base}

\begin{theorem}[Bits vs atoms --- claims 1--4, 13]
\label{thm:bits_atoms}
The fundamental asymmetry:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Information fiber} (genetic designs, synthesis protocols,
    model weights) is freely copyable. Cannot be contained by guarding
    a single copy --- copies propagate at the speed of communication.
  \item \textbf{Physical base} (DNA synthesis reagents, biological
    substrates, laboratory equipment) is rivalrous, trackable, and
    gateable. Has physical scarcity.
\end{itemize}

Security must live at the base layer when the fiber cannot be contained:
\[
  \text{containable}(F_{\text{info}}) = \text{false} \implies
    \text{security\_layer} = B_{\text{physical}}
\]
``Gate the atoms, not the bits'' is the correct identification of which
bundle layer admits effective control. Cryptographic laterality (from
Part~2) doesn't apply because the dangerous thing is small copyable
information, not a networked cognitive process.
\end{theorem}

\section{Biology vs cybersecurity}

\begin{proposition}[Asymmetric defensibility --- claim 5]
\label{prop:bio_cyber}
Biology and cybersecurity have fundamentally different defense profiles:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Cyber:} Defenders can patch software instantly. The
    attack surface is human-made and rewritable. Defense is cheap
    relative to attack (once the patch exists).
  \item \textbf{Bio:} Cannot patch a human body. Vaccines are slow
    (months to years), partial (incomplete protection), and limited
    (fraction of population reached). The attack surface is our own
    evolved biology --- not rewritable.
\end{itemize}
This asymmetry means cyber defense strategies don't transfer directly
to biosecurity. Bio-specific defenses are needed.
\end{proposition}

\section{Decentralized verification as distributed fiber inspection}

\begin{proposition}[Distributed inspection --- claims 6--8, 14]
\label{prop:distributed}
A central verification database is a trivial bundle (single inspector,
single point of failure). A distributed verification network is a
non-trivial bundle:
\[
  E_{\text{verification}} \not\cong B \times F
  \quad \text{(globally non-trivial)}
\]
Each verification node inspects the fiber from its own vantage point:
sequence screening, identity verification, provenance checking,
reputation assessment. No single inspector sees everything;
collectively they cover the full fiber.

The non-trivial topology is the security feature: no single compromise
defeats the system. Device identities (cryptographic key sealed in chip)
and operator credentials (verifiable without central registry) provide
the local trivializations; the transition functions between them are
the verification protocols.
\end{proposition}

\section{Scoped reputation as multi-fiber trust}

\begin{definition}[Multi-fiber trust --- claims 9, 15]
\label{def:trust}
Trust is not a scalar but a multi-dimensional fiber:
\[
  F_{\text{trust}} = F_{\text{scope}} \times F_{\text{temporal}}
    \times F_{\text{delegation}} \times F_{\text{context}}
\]
\begin{itemize}
  \item $F_{\text{scope}}$: what the entity is trusted for (academic
    research, pathogen work, industrial synthesis).
  \item $F_{\text{temporal}}$: temporal decay (recent clean record vs
    ancient certification).
  \item $F_{\text{delegation}}$: delegation chain (institution vouches
    for individual).
  \item $F_{\text{context}}$: context specificity (trusted for this
    type of synthesis in this jurisdiction).
\end{itemize}
Collapsing to a single ``trustworthy/untrustworthy'' scalar is
scalar collapse --- losing the structural information essential for
biosecurity decisions.
\end{definition}

\section{Neural-symbolic screening as generate-and-verify}

\begin{proposition}[Generate-and-verify for biology --- claims 10--11, 16]
\label{prop:screening}
The screening architecture follows the generate-and-verify pattern:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Generate (LLM).} Pattern recognition identifies
    potentially dangerous sequences from the vast space of possible
    designs.
  \item \textbf{Verify (PLN).} Uncertain reasoning about intent,
    context, provenance. Strength truth values are essential because
    most sequences are dual-use --- the screening must reason about
    probability of malicious intent, not make binary classifications.
\end{enumerate}
Distributed screening nodes ensure no single entity holds the master
watchlist. Each node contributes partial screening from its specialization;
the collective achieves coverage.
\end{proposition}

\section{Distributed biosurveillance as immune system}

\begin{proposition}[Immune system architecture --- claim 12, 17]
\label{prop:immune}
The biosurveillance system is an immune system, not a watchtower:
many independent detectors (environmental DNA monitors, wastewater
sensors, clinical anomaly detectors) sampling the environment, each
contributing partial information, collectively achieving coverage
no single detector could.

This is exactly distributed fiber inspection applied to the
environmental/health domain: many observers sampling the same
underlying biological threat landscape from different vantage points.
\end{proposition}

\begin{conjecture}[Physical-layer security sufficiency]
Conjecture: when the information fiber is uncontainable (the dangerous
capability is small, copyable, and un-recallable), the security floor
achievable through physical-layer gating alone is bounded below by:
\[
  S_{\text{floor}} \geq 1 - \prod_{j \in \text{gates}}
  (1 - s_j)
\]
where $s_j$ is the effectiveness of gate $j$ (reagent supply control,
synthesis screening, equipment tracking). The bound is tight when the
gates are independent --- another instance of the anti-scalar-collapse
principle: multiple independent gates provide multiplicative security
improvement, while a single centralized gate provides only additive
improvement and is vulnerable to single-point failure.
\end{conjecture}

\section{d-calculus formulation (Hyperseed v2)}

\begin{proposition}[Information-physical asymmetry curvature --- claim 18]
\label{prop:info_phys_curv}
\[
  \|F_\nabla(\text{info}, \text{physical})\| = \text{containability
    asymmetry}
\]
High curvature: strong asymmetry (information freely copyable, physical
materials scarce --- the actual situation). This high curvature is
\emph{why} ``gate the atoms not the bits'' is the correct strategy:
the curvature identifies where control is possible.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Verification coverage curvature --- claim 19]
\label{prop:coverage_curv}
\[
  \|F_{\nabla_{\text{verification}}}\| = \text{coverage quality of
    distributed inspection}
\]
High curvature: good coverage (many independent inspectors, diverse
vantage points, few blind spots). Low curvature: poor coverage
(correlated inspectors, shared blind spots --- effective single point
of failure despite nominal distribution).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Reputation fiber curvature --- claim 20]
\label{prop:rep_curv}
\[
  \|F_{\nabla_{\text{reputation}}}\| = \text{trust dimensionality
    and context-sensitivity}
\]
High curvature: trust is genuinely multi-dimensional (different scopes,
temporal decay, delegation structure --- rich fiber). Low curvature:
trust is approximately scalar (collapsed to a single number despite
nominal dimensions --- effective scalar collapse).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Screening holonomy --- claim 21]
\label{prop:screening_hol}
A loop through the screening pipeline $\gamma$: sequence submission
$\to$ pattern detection $\to$ intent reasoning $\to$ provenance check
$\to$ decision $\to$ feedback produces holonomy:
\[
  \operatorname{Hol}_\gamma(\Gamma_{\text{screening}}) =
    \text{screening drift per cycle}
\]
\begin{itemize}
  \item Low holonomy: screening stays calibrated (stable false
    positive/negative rates, reliable decisions).
  \item High holonomy: screening drifts (rates shift, recalibration
    needed, evolving threats outpace the model).
\end{itemize}
The distributed design minimizes screening holonomy through diverse,
independent nodes that correct each other's drift.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Biosurveillance gradient --- claim 22]
\label{prop:surv_grad}
\[
  \nabla_{\text{surveillance}} = \nabla\left(\text{detection\_sensitivity}
    \times \text{geographic\_coverage} \times
    \text{temporal\_resolution} \times \text{pathogen\_breadth}\right)
\]
Steep gradient: uneven coverage (some regions/pathogens well-covered,
others blind --- dangerous gaps). Flat gradient: uniform coverage
(ideal immune system with no blind spots). The design goal is to
flatten this gradient: achieve uniform detection capability across
all dimensions of the threat space.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Attack surface curvature --- claim 23]
\label{prop:attack_curv}
\[
  \|F_\nabla(\text{bio}, \text{cyber})\| = \text{fundamental difference
    in defensibility}
\]
High curvature: biology and cybersecurity are fundamentally different
in defensibility (can't patch a human body vs.\ can patch software).
This curvature quantifies why bio-specific defenses are needed ---
cyber defense strategies don't transfer directly to biosecurity.
\end{proposition}
